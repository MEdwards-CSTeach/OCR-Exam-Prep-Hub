Exam tips and improving your grade

OCR A Level Computer Science • H446

From A to A*

Go beyond the specification. A* students are consistent across both papers and bring depth from wider reading into their answers.

The big idea

Read around the subject. Real examples and deeper understanding make an answer stand out from one that only uses the spec.

What makes the difference

  • Consistency: an A* needs a very high total, so you can’t afford to drop easy marks anywhere.
  • Precision: every term used correctly, every algorithm working first time.
  • Depth: answers that show understanding beyond the textbook.
  • Real examples: evidence from wider reading to support arguments.
  • Thinking like a computer scientist: trade-offs, efficiency, edge cases.

Important: wider reading adds to the spec – it doesn’t replace it. Answer the question first using spec content, then add depth. A clever example in the wrong place earns nothing.

Before and after

Example 1

Adding depth with real evidence

Question: A bank plans to use an automated system to decide loan applications. Discuss the ethical issues. [9] (extract on one issue)

A-grade answer Good – accurate and applied

Automated systems can be biased if they are trained on biased data. This would be unfair to some customers, so the bank should check the system for bias.

A*-grade answer Exceptional – depth and evidence

Automated systems learn patterns from historical data, so they can repeat past bias. For example, a 2019 study by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology found that many facial recognition algorithms were less accurate for some demographic groups. If the bank’s past loan decisions were unfair to some groups, the system could repeat that unfairness faster and at a far larger scale than a human adviser. Because customers may not be able to find out why they were refused, the bank should audit the system regularly and let customers ask for a human review.

What made the difference:

  • Explains how bias happens, not just that it can.
  • Supports the point with real evidence from wider reading.
  • Spots a further issue (scale and explainability) and links it to a solution.
Example 2

Thinking like a computer scientist

Question: Explain why quick sort is usually chosen over bubble sort for sorting a large data set. [4]

A-grade answer Full marks

Quick sort has an average time complexity of O(n log n), while bubble sort is O(n²). For a large data set, n² grows much faster, so quick sort will be much quicker.

A*-grade thinking Same marks – deeper understanding

…as the A-grade answer, plus awareness that quick sort’s worst case is O(n²) – for example, when the data is already sorted and the first item is used as the pivot. Choosing a better pivot (such as the middle item) avoids this. Bubble sort can finish in O(n) on data that is already sorted, so the best choice depends on the data.

What made the difference:

  • Knows the limits of the “right” answer, not just the answer.
  • This depth matters in “evaluate” questions about choosing algorithms.
  • Answer the question asked first – then add depth where it earns marks.

Videos and courses

  • Computerphile – university computer scientists explain topics, from encryption to AI.
  • Ben Eater – builds an 8-bit computer from scratch. Brilliant for 1.1 and 1.4.3.
  • Harvard CS50 – a free introductory university course.

Practise and keep up to date

  • Project Euler – maths-based programming challenges.
  • Isaac Computer Science – harder questions on every topic.
  • Information Commissioner’s Office – current UK data protection law.
  • BBC Technology news – real examples for ethics questions.

Best revision techniques for this step

  • Teach it – test whether you understand in depth
  • Planning extended answers – build in evidence from wider reading
  • Condensing notes – keep a notebook of real examples by topic
  • Concept maps – connect wider reading to spec topics

These suit this step best, but use any technique that works for you.
