Exam structure

You take three components. Two are written exams. One is a programming project.

Component 01

Paper 1: Computer systems

  • 140 marks
  • 2 hours 30 minutes
  • Written paper
  • No calculators
  • 40% of the A level

Component 02

Paper 2: Algorithms and programming

  • 140 marks
  • 2 hours 30 minutes
  • Written paper
  • No calculators
  • 40% of the A level

Component 03 or 04

Programming project

  • 70 marks
  • Non-exam assessment
  • Marked by your teacher, checked by OCR
  • 20% of the A level

What each paper looks like

Paper 1: Computer systems

  • Answer all the questions.
  • A mix of short answer, longer answer and high tariff questions.
  • Some longer questions test the quality of your extended writing.
  • Questions can ask you to follow and correct algorithms and programs.
  • Covers topics 1.1 to 1.5.

Paper 2: Algorithms and programming

  • Answer all questions in Section A and Section B.
  • Section A: writing algorithms, programming techniques, computational methods and problem solving. Includes some short questions.
  • Section B: starts with a scenario. The questions that follow use it. Mostly high tariff problem solving.
  • Covers topics 2.1 to 2.3, and builds on Paper 1.

Tip for Section B: read the scenario twice before you start. Underline key facts – you will need them in later answers.

Assessment objectives (AOs)

Every exam question tests one or more of these three skills. Good revision practises all three, not just remembering facts.

AO1

Know it

Show knowledge and understanding of computer science principles and concepts.

Key words: state, identify, define, describe

“State the purpose of the MAR.”

AO2

Apply it

Use what you know in a new situation, and analyse problems in computational terms.

Key words: explain, calculate, convert, trace, show

“Convert −45 to 8-bit two’s complement.”

AO3

Judge and create it

Design, program and evaluate solutions. Make reasoned judgements and reach a conclusion.

Key words: discuss, evaluate, justify, design, write an algorithm

“Discuss whether a school should use cloud storage.”

How much each AO is worth

Percentage of the whole A level (rounded).

ComponentAO1AO2AO3Total
Paper 1: Computer systems21%9%10%40%
Paper 2: Algorithms and programming15%18%7%40%
Programming project0%3%17%20%

What this means for revision:

  • Paper 1: learn the facts and key terms well, then practise “discuss” questions.
  • Paper 2: practise doing – tracing, writing and fixing algorithms. Reading notes is not enough.

Paper 1: Computer systems

1.1 The characteristics of contemporary processors, input, output and storage devices

1.1.1 Structure and function of the processor

What you need to know:

  1. The Arithmetic and Logic Unit (ALU), Control Unit and registers (PC, ACC, MAR, MDR, CIR). Buses: data, address and control, and how this relates to assembly language programs.
  2. The Fetch-Decode-Execute cycle, including its effects on registers.
  3. Factors affecting CPU performance: clock speed, number of cores, cache.
  4. The use of pipelining in a processor to improve efficiency.
  5. Von Neumann, Harvard and contemporary processor architecture.

1.1.2 Types of processor

What you need to know:

  1. The differences between and uses of CISC and RISC processors.
  2. GPUs and their uses (including those not related to graphics).
  3. Multicore and parallel systems.

What you need to know:

  1. How different input, output and storage devices can be applied to the solution of different problems.

1.2 Software and software development

1.2.1 Systems software

What you need to know:

  1. The need for, function and purpose of operating systems.
  2. Memory management (paging, segmentation and virtual memory).
  3. Interrupts, the role of interrupts and Interrupt Service Routines (ISR), role within the Fetch-Decode-Execute cycle.
  4. Scheduling: round robin, first come first served, multi-level feedback queues, shortest job first and shortest remaining time.
  5. Distributed, embedded, multi-tasking, multi-user and real time operating systems.
  6. BIOS.
  7. Device drivers.
  8. Virtual machines, including executing intermediate code or running an operating system within another.

1.4 Data types, data structures and algorithms

1.4.1 Data types

What you need to know:

  1. Primitive data types: integer, real/floating point, character, string and Boolean.
  2. Represent positive integers in binary.
  3. Use of sign and magnitude and two's complement to represent negative numbers in binary.
  4. Addition and subtraction of binary integers.
  5. Represent positive integers in hexadecimal.
  6. Convert positive integers between binary, hexadecimal and denary.
  7. Representation and normalisation of floating point numbers in binary.
  8. Floating point arithmetic, positive and negative numbers, addition and subtraction.
  9. Bitwise manipulation and masks: shifts, combining with AND, OR and XOR.
  10. How character sets (ASCII and Unicode) are used to represent text.

What you need to know:

  1. Define problems using Boolean logic.
  2. Manipulate Boolean expressions, including the use of Karnaugh maps to simplify them.
  3. Use De Morgan's Laws, distribution, association, commutation and double negation to derive or simplify statements.
  4. Logic gate diagrams and truth tables.
  5. The logic associated with D type flip flops, half and full adders.

What you need to know:

  3. Global and local variables.

What you need to know:

  1. Features that make a problem solvable by computational methods.

  3. Problem decomposition.
  4. Use of divide and conquer.

2.3 Algorithms

2.3.1 Algorithms

What you need to know:

  1. Analysis and design of algorithms for a given situation.
  2. The suitability of different algorithms for a given task and data set, in terms of execution time and space.
  3. Measures and methods to determine efficiency, Big O notation (constant, linear, polynomial, exponential and logarithmic complexity).
  4. Comparison of the complexity of algorithms.
  5. Algorithms for the main data structures (stacks, queues, trees, linked lists, depth-first (post-order) and breadth-first traversal of trees).
  6. Standard algorithms: bubble sort, insertion sort, merge sort, quick sort, Dijkstra's shortest path, A*, binary search and linear search.

Programming project (non-exam assessment)

You solve a problem of your choice with a program, and write a report. It is worth 70 marks (20%).

SectionWhat you doMarks
AnalysisIdentify the problem, the stakeholders, and research similar solutions.10
DesignBreak the problem down and design the solution and tests.15
DevelopmentBuild and test the solution in stages.25
EvaluationTest against your success criteria and evaluate the solution.20
