2.1.1 Thinking abstractly

Removing detail to build a model: what abstraction is, why we need it, how a model differs from reality, and how to design one.

What is abstraction?

Abstraction is removing or hiding unnecessary detail so you can focus on what matters to the problem. The result is a model: a simpler version of reality.

The nature of abstraction (a)

  • Representational: keep only what you need – a Tube map, a sat nav map.
  • Generalisation: group things by what they share – all enemies move and take damage.
  • Procedural: use a subroutine without knowing how it works – print("Hi").
  • Data abstraction: use a stack through push and pop, not how it is stored.
  • Layers: each layer hides the one below – the TCP/IP stack.

The need for abstraction (b)

  • Problems become less complex and easier to understand.
  • Less data to store and process, so it runs faster and uses less memory.
  • Quicker to design, write and test.
  • People can use a tool without knowing how it works.

Abstraction vs reality (c)

AbstractionHow it differs from realitySo what?
Tube mapNot to scale: bends straightened, distances changedSome stations are closer on foot than they look

Difference → consequence → effect: say how the model differs, what that changes, and what it means for the user.

Devise an abstract model (d)

  1. Problem: what question must the model answer? How long do students wait with 1, 2 or 3 tills?
  2. Keep: details that change the answer. Arrivals per minute, time to serve, number of tills.
  3. Remove: everything else. Names, what they eat, tray colour.
  4. Represent: how the kept parts are stored. Each till is a queue; time moves in 1-minute steps.

Too much detail

Slower, uses more memory, harder to build. A sat nav storing every tree.

Too little detail

The model gives wrong answers. A flight simulator with no bad weather.

Watch out for:

  • “It makes it simpler” with no detail from the scenario.
  • Mixing up abstraction (taking detail away) and decomposition (breaking the problem down).
  • Removing a detail the problem actually needs, such as the weather in a traffic simulation.
  • Naming a detail to remove but not saying why it can go.

Quick quiz – marked for you

1. Abstraction is… AO1

A is decomposition, C is concurrency, D is caching.

2. A taxi fare app stores the colour of the taxi and the song playing. This is… AO2

Colour and song don’t change the fare.

3. A flight simulator for training pilots has no bad weather. This is… AO2

Pilots must practise landing in bad weather, so this detail is needed.

4. Which detail should a train departure board keep? AO2

Passengers need the platform. The slope matters to a driver simulator; age matters to a price calculator.

5. Which is a benefit of abstraction? AO1

Less data means faster processing and less memory – and quicker to build.

6. A disease model assumes everyone meets everyone else equally. Why might its predictions be wrong? AO3

Too little detail: the difference from reality changes the result.

Exam-style questions – mark them yourself

Write your answer, then open Mark your answer. Tick the mark points you hit, read the model answer and use the tips to improve. See the 2.1 exam tips for more help.

AO1[2 marks]

A zoo app finds the shortest walking route between enclosures. Describe what is meant by abstraction.

Mark your answer

Tick each mark point your answer includes.

Model answer: Abstraction is removing unnecessary detail from a problem so that you can focus on the parts that matter, such as the paths between enclosures.

How to improve a weak answer:

  • Two parts: what you do (remove detail) and why (focus on what matters).
  • “Making it simpler” alone is only half.
AO2[4 marks]

Identify two details that could be removed from the zoo route app, and explain why each can be removed.

Mark your answer

Tick each mark point your answer includes.

Model answer: The names of the animals can be removed because they do not change which path is shortest. The colours of the enclosures can be removed because they do not affect the walking distance.

How to improve a weak answer:

  • One mark for the detail, one for the reason – every time.
  • The reason must say why it doesn’t affect this problem: the route.
AO3[3 marks]

The app treats every path as a straight line. Explain how this abstraction differs from reality and the effect this could have on a visitor.

Mark your answer

Tick each mark point your answer includes.

Model answer: Real paths bend and have slopes or steps, so the real distance is longer than the app calculates. A visitor might arrive later than the app said, or find steps they can’t use with a pushchair.

How to improve a weak answer:

  • Use the chain: difference → consequence → effect on the user.
  • Three marks means three linked steps.
