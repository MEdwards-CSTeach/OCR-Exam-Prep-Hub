Revision technique

Blurting (brain dump)

Write down everything you can remember about a topic, from memory, then check what you missed.

At a glance

  • Time: 15 minutes
  • Best for: Checking a whole topic quickly
  • You need: Blank paper and two coloured pens

Why it works

Blurting shows you exactly what you have forgotten. Instead of rereading things you already know, you spend your time on your gaps. It is retrieval practice, so the act of remembering also makes the knowledge stronger.

How to do it

  1. Pick a topic and read your notes on it for 5–10 minutes.
  2. Close everything. On a blank page, write everything you remember in 5 minutes. Any order is fine.
  3. Open your notes and check. Add anything you missed or got wrong in a different colour.
  4. Focus on the coloured bits – these are your gaps.
  5. Blurt again in a few days. Aim for less colour each time.

Best topics for this technique

  • 1.2.1 Systems software
  • 1.3.3 Networks
  • 1.3.4 Web technologies
  • 1.5.1 Computing legislation
  • 1.5.2 Moral and ethical issues
  • 2.1.1 Computational thinking – all of 2.1

These topics suit this technique well, but you can use it for any topic.

Tip: Blurt a topic before you revise it as well as after. The first blurt shows you where to start.

Examples

Example 1

A student’s blurt on computing legislation

1.5.1

Black = remembered from memory. Green = added after checking the notes.

From memory (5 minutes)

  • Data Protection Act 1998 – personal data must be kept secure and accurate, and not kept longer than needed
  • Computer Misuse Act 1990 – illegal to access a computer without permission (hacking)
  • CMA – also illegal to spread viruses
  • Copyright Act – illegal to copy software, music or films without permission

Added after checking

  • Full name: Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988
  • CMA has three offences: unauthorised access; access with intent to commit further offences; unauthorised modification of data
  • DPA: people have rights, e.g. to see data held about them; data must only be used for the stated purpose
  • Forgot completely: Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 – lets authorities intercept communications and demand access to encrypted data

What this tells the student: revise RIPA first, then the three CMA offences.
